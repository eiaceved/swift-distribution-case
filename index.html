Swift Distribution

A distributor known for great service has watched its revenue fall three years in a row while cheaper rivals take customers. Find the root cause of the profit decline, and a way back to where profits used to be.

01

How this case works

This is an interviewer-led case. The interviewer asks questions in order: build a framework, read an exhibit, do some math, then give a recommendation.

Each question has one job. The framework shows you can organize a problem. The exhibit shows you can spot what matters in data. The math shows you can stay accurate under pressure.

The workshop's hints

From the case slides, in short

  • Take time to organize your thoughts before you answer.
  • Lay out an overall approach before diving into details.
  • Write down important facts as you hear them.
  • Ask the interviewer to explain anything that isn't clear.
  • Look for trends in the data that aren't obvious at first glance.

What a strong answer shows

The same in every question

  • A logical structure, even if your guess turns out wrong.
  • Clean, simple math, said out loud.
  • A hypothesis you test with the data.
  • The answer first, then the reasons.

Ten terms to drop naturally

Distributor

A middleman that buys in bulk from manufacturers, stores the goods, and delivers smaller amounts to retailers.

COGS

Cost of goods sold: what Swift pays for the products it resells. It rises and falls with sales.

OpEx

Operating expenses: day-to-day running costs, like sales teams and customer support.

Fixed costs

Costs that stay about the same when sales change, like warehouse leases and technology.

Profit margin

Profit as a share of revenue. 10 million of profit on 100 million of revenue is a 10 percent margin.

Market share

Swift's sales divided by the whole market. 40 million out of a 200 million market is 20 percent.

Sales mix

How revenue splits across segments or customer types. A shift in mix can change profit even if total sales hold.

Key buying factors

What customers care about most when choosing a supplier, like price or service.

Cost to serve

What it costs to deliver to and support one customer. Fast delivery and great support raise it.

Harvey ball

A circle filled a quarter, half, or three-quarters to show low, medium, or high. Consulting slides use them a lot.

02

The case prompt

Interviewer

Our client is Swift Distribution, a national distributor of consumer goods that serves retail clients across North America. Swift buys products in bulk from manufacturers, stores them, and delivers smaller quantities to retailers like supermarkets and specialty stores. It often adds services like inventory management and transportation.

Swift carries everything from electronics to household products. It's known for industry-leading service: fast delivery and excellent customer support.

But for the past three years, Swift's revenue has dropped every year. Lower-cost competitors are winning customers with cheaper prices, even though they can't match Swift's service. The CEO has asked us to find the root cause of the decline in profits, and to get profits back to historic levels.

Your move

Restate the question in one sentence before you do anything else.

Strong answer

"So Swift's profits have been falling as cheaper competitors take customers, and the CEO wants us to find the root cause and get profits back to where they were. Is that right?"

Interviewer: "Yes, exactly." Notice the restatement names both parts: find the cause, and fix it.

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. Pick ones whose answers would change your approach.

Strong questions, with practice answers

  • What does "historic levels" mean in dollars?
    About 14 million dollars of profit a year, where it was two years ago.
  • Who are the lower-cost competitors?
    Newer distributors that offer fewer services at lower prices. They can't match Swift's delivery speed or support.
  • Is the decline across all product types, or some?
    Good question. We have a breakdown by segment that we'll show you shortly.
  • Any constraints on the solution?
    The CEO doesn't want to give up Swift's reputation for great service.

These answers were written for practice; the workshop didn't share them. In a real interview, write down every number you hear.

04

Question 1: build the framework

Interviewer

What factors would you consider when investigating why Swift is experiencing a decline in profits?

Your move

Take 60 to 90 seconds. Start with the overall split, profit equals revenue minus costs, then add what else could matter. Write it down, then walk through it out loud.

Strong framework, close to the workshop's model answer

What could be driving Swift's decline in profits?
1Revenue: is it falling?
  • Products: which categories earn the most, and did price or units change?
  • Customers: sales by customer type, satisfaction, fewer customers or new needs?
  • Operations: changes to delivery or suppliers that hurt service?
2Costs: are they rising?
  • Type of cost: fixed, like leases and technology, or variable, like trucking and fuel?
  • Market factors: a recession or inflation pushing costs up?
  • Suppliers: who they are, and what they charge?
3Other: what else matters?
  • Competitors: their prices, market share, and reach?
  • Organization: changes to Swift's teams or key departments?
  • Technology: how Swift manages distribution, and how that affects sales?

Say it like this

"Profit is revenue minus costs, so I'd check both sides, then look at outside factors. On revenue, I'd look at products, which categories earn the most and whether price or volume changed; customers, which types buy from us, and whether we're losing them; and operations, whether anything changed in how we deliver. On costs, I'd split fixed costs, like leases and technology, from variable costs, like trucking, and check market factors and supplier prices. Then other factors: competitors' pricing and share, any changes inside Swift, and technology. Since we know cheaper rivals are taking customers, my hypothesis is that revenue is the main problem, so I'd like to start with revenue by product segment."

Why it works

  • It starts with the overall split, revenue and costs, before details, which is what the workshop's hint asks for.
  • It has a home for every idea: the "other" bucket catches competitors and internal changes.
  • It ends with a hypothesis and a data request, which moves the case forward.

The most common mistake: listing 15 random factors with no buckets. Three buckets with three sub-points each sounds organized, and it's easy to remember under pressure.

Framework practice: sort the factors

A quick drill to build the habit. Put each factor in the bucket where it belongs in a profit framework: Revenue, Costs, or Other. Then check your answers.

    06

    Question 2: read the exhibit

    Interviewer

    To dig into what's driving the decline, the team gathered revenue by type of goods, Swift's annual costs, and information on market size and customer demand. What insights can you draw to help Swift prioritize opportunities to improve profits? Feel free to offer hypotheses.

    Which finding should you lead with?

    Total costs actually fell, from 96 million to 90. The drop is in revenue: from 110 million to 100. Electronics fell from 45 to 30, down a third. Household held at 30, and specialty grew from 35 to 40. So the problem is concentrated in one segment, and that's where the cheaper rivals are winning.

    Now the less obvious trend. What does the cost chart tell you?

    COGS stays at about 58 percent of revenue, so it falls when sales fall. But OpEx stayed at 15 million, and fixed costs crept up from 16 to 17 million. Revenue fell 10 million and costs only fell 6, so profit dropped from 14 million to 10, and margin from about 13 percent to 10. The costs aren't the cause, but they make the revenue drop hurt more.

    And the so-what from the segment table?

    Electronics is the biggest market, but Swift's margin there is low and buyers weigh price heavily, so cutting prices would shrink an already thin margin. Specialty buyers care most about service, and Swift already has about 20 percent of that market. Specialty orders are also twice as big, 2,000 dollars versus 1,000, so each delivery earns more. Household has the highest margin, but its buyers care most about price, so it could be the next target for cheaper rivals.

    Then

    Synthesize all three parts of the exhibit in three or four sentences, ending with a hypothesis.

    Say it like this

    "Three things stand out. First, revenue fell 10 million, and it's all electronics, down from 45 to 30, while specialty grew. Second, costs didn't fall as fast: COGS tracks sales, but operating and fixed costs stayed at about 31 to 32 million, so margin fell from about 13 percent to 10. Third, the segment table explains why: electronics is a big, low-margin market where price matters, which is exactly where cheaper rivals win, while specialty buyers value service, Swift's strength. My hypothesis is that Swift should stop fighting on price in electronics, grow household and specialty, and trim the costs that didn't shrink."

    The workshop's hint was to look for trends that aren't obvious. Here, that's the sticky operating and fixed costs, the bigger specialty orders, and household buyers being price-focused despite the high margin.

    Practice assumptions. Margins on extra sales already include the extra cost of serving those customers.

    Your move

    Size each option: extra sales times margin, or the cost saved. Then pick the combination that closes the 4 million gap without hurting service.

    1. A · Grow specialty$10M × 10%+$1.0M

    Quick check: why is option C, cutting electronics prices, the weakest?

    Matching cheaper rivals means fighting on their terms. Swift would earn about 2 cents on each new dollar of sales, and give up margin on what it already sells. Everyday version: a coffee shop that cuts its price to match the gas station sells more cups but earns less on every cup, including the ones it would have sold anyway.

    Then

    Summarize the math in two sentences.

    Say it like this

    "Cutting the costs that didn't shrink is worth about 2.5 million, and growing household, the highest-margin segment, adds about 1.5 million, which together close the 4 million gap. Growing specialty adds another million and plays to Swift's service strength, while cutting electronics prices adds almost nothing and puts the margin on existing sales at risk."

    Score the ideas

    Interviewer

    Here are six ideas the team came up with. Score each for ease and impact, from 1 to 5. Which three would you start with?

    Your move

    Score each idea out loud with a one-line reason, then name your top three.

    The team's scores, with reasons

      Say it like this

      "I'd start with three. Cut the operating and fixed costs that didn't shrink with sales, because it's in Swift's control and worth about 2.5 million. Grow household, the highest-margin segment. And win more specialty customers, where service matters most. I'd avoid matching rivals' prices in electronics; that's fighting on their terms."

      Which three come first?

      C, B, and A score highest and match the math from Question 3. D, a no-frills tier for electronics, is worth testing later. E, matching prices, is easy but destroys margin. F, exiting electronics, would lose 30 million of sales that help pay the fixed costs.

      10

      The recommendation

      Interviewer

      The CEO just walked in. You have 60 seconds. What's driving the decline, and what should Swift do?

      Your move

      Lead with the answer. Then three reasons, the main risk, and next steps.

      Strong answer

      Answer3 reasonsRiskNext steps

      "Swift's profit fell from 14 million to 10 million because electronics sales dropped by a third to cheaper rivals, while operating and fixed costs didn't shrink. To get back to 14 million, Swift should stop fighting on price in electronics and play to its strengths: cut about 2.5 million of sticky costs, grow household, and win more specialty customers."

      "First, the decline is concentrated. Electronics fell from 45 million to 30, in a low-margin market where buyers weigh price. Matching rivals there would earn almost nothing."

      "Second, costs are sticky. COGS falls with sales, but operating and fixed costs stayed at about 32 million. Bringing them back down is worth about 2.5 million, and it's fully in Swift's control."

      "Third, Swift's strengths point to other segments. Household earns the highest margin and adds about 1.5 million with 2 more points of share. Specialty buyers value service most, and it adds another million."

      "The main risk is that electronics keeps shrinking and leaves fixed costs spread over fewer sales. So I'd also test a lower-cost delivery option for price-focused electronics buyers. Next steps: run a cost review of OpEx and fixed costs, build account plans for the top household and specialty prospects, and design the electronics test."

      The 90-day plan

      Days 1–30Cut
      • Review every OpEx and fixed cost line.
      • Find the 10 percent of OpEx that doesn't help service.
      Days 31–60Grow
      • Target the top household and specialty prospects.
      • Use service, not price, as the pitch.
      Days 61–90Test
      • Pilot a lower-cost delivery option for electronics buyers.
      • Track profit monthly against the 14 million goal.

      Indicators to track

      • OpEx plus fixed costs. Target: back to about 29.5 million.
      • Household revenue. Target: up 10 million, to 40 million.
      • Specialty market share. Target: from 20 percent toward 25.
      • Profit margin. Target: back to about 13 percent.

      Drills: framework reps and math warm-up

      Short reps to build speed. For each prompt, take 60 seconds to say three buckets with two or three points each, then compare.

      Framework reps

      1. A bakery chain's profits are falling. What would you look at?

      Revenue

      Number of customers, visits per customer, and spend per visit. Which products sell, and did prices change?

      Costs

      Ingredients like flour and butter, staff wages, and rent. Which went up?

      Outside factors

      New competitors nearby, changing tastes like low-sugar diets, and delivery apps.

      Hypothesis

      "Ingredient costs probably rose faster than prices, so I'd start with costs per item."

      2. A gym chain wants to open in a new city. Should it?

      Market

      How many people would join, how fast the market is growing, and who the competitors are.

      Customers

      Who they are, what they'd pay each month, and what they care about.

      Economics

      Cost to open, members needed to break even, and payback time.

      Ability to win and risks

      Brand recognition, finding good locations, and how rivals might respond.

      3. A hospital's emergency room wait times doubled. Why?

      Demand

      More patients arriving, sicker patients, or a nearby clinic that closed.

      Capacity

      Fewer doctors and nurses on shift, or fewer beds available.

      Process

      Slow check-in or triage, waiting on tests, or patients stuck waiting to be admitted.

      Hypothesis

      "If arrivals are flat, the problem is inside: staffing or a bottleneck in the process."

      4. A distributor like Swift is thinking about buying a smaller rival. Should it?

      Market

      Is the rival's segment growing and profitable?

      Target

      Its customers, margins, and service quality. Does it fit Swift's brand?

      Synergies

      Shared warehouses and trucks, and selling more to each other's customers.

      Price and risks

      Is the price fair, and can the two companies be combined without hurting service?

      Math warm-up: tricks from the workshop

      Adding

      Pair numbers that make tens first. 7 plus 13 plus 6 plus 4 is 20 plus 10, which is 30.

      Subtracting from round numbers

      For 1,000 minus 437, do 999 minus 437, which is 562, then add 1 to get 563.

      Multiplying

      Split it: 16 times 7 is 10 times 7 plus 6 times 7, so 70 plus 42 is 112. Set zeros aside: 30 times 400 is 3 times 4, then add three zeros, so 12,000.

      Percents and dividing

      Find 10 percent first: 30 percent of 250 is 3 times 25, or 75. 25 percent is a quarter. To divide by 5, double it and divide by 10.
